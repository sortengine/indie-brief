Good morning, this is Indie Brief for Monday 16 November.

Poland's documentary sector is celebrating a major milestone. Deadline reports that Millennium Docs Against Gravity has officially become the world's second biggest documentary film festival by audience numbers. The festival, which took place in May this year, recorded one hundred and eighty thousand admissions, putting it just behind IDFA in Amsterdam. That's significant recognition for the independent documentary community in Central Europe.

In festival news from Greece, Jacqueline Lentzou's feature A Day In The Life Of Jo: Chapter Phaedra has taken the top prize at Thessaloniki Film Festival. The film won the Golden Alexander Award for Best Feature Film, which comes with a ten thousand euro cash prize. It's another strong result for independent European filmmaking on the festival circuit.

Closer to home, Kirk Jones's I Swear has led the winners at BAFTA Scotland. The film, which tells the true story of John Davidson, a Scottish man with severe Tourette syndrome, won three awards including Best Film and Best Director. Actor Robert Aramayo also won Best Actor Film for the picture.

On the documentary side, Modern Times Review highlights Ghost in the Machine, an investigative essay documentary that explores artificial intelligence, examining the racist and eugenicist foundations at the core of AI technology. It's a timely examination of a technology increasingly relevant to independent producers.

Finally, as the entertainment industry continues to reflect on safeguarding issues, Deadline reports that nearly four hundred British creatives, including actors Natalie Dormer, Richard Armitage and Susan Wokoma, have signed an open letter calling for an end to what they describe as a culture of open secrets in the industry.
